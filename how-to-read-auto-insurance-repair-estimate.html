How to Read an Auto Insurance Repair Estimate

Receiving an auto insurance repair estimate can feel like trying to read a completely different language.

It is usually a multi-page document filled with confusing acronyms, decimal numbers representing time, and strange line items you never knew existed on your car. But understanding this document is the key to knowing exactly how your vehicle is being repaired and why your claim is progressing the way it is.

Once you understand a few basic terms, reading an estimate becomes surprisingly straightforward.

Decoding the Line Items

Every line on an estimate generally represents a specific action or part required to fix the damage. Here is what the main columns and terms mean:

  • Labour (Body vs. Paint): Labour is usually split into different categories, each with its own hourly rate. "Body labour" covers physically fixing or replacing parts. "Paint labour" covers prepping and spraying the panels. The time is usually listed in decimal hours (e.g., 1.5 means one and a half hours).
  • OEM Parts: "Original Equipment Manufacturer" parts are made directly by your car's manufacturer (like Ford, Honda, or Toyota). They are brand new and generally the most expensive option.
  • Aftermarket Parts: These are brand-new parts made by third-party companies, not the vehicle manufacturer. Insurance policies frequently mandate the use of aftermarket parts for cosmetics (like a bumper cover) to keep repair costs down.
  • LKQ (Like Kind and Quality) / Recycled: These are used parts salvaged from another vehicle of the same make and model. They are original manufacturer parts, just not brand new.
  • Blend Times: If a shop replaces your front door, they cannot just paint the new door; the fresh paint won't perfectly match the older, sun-faded fender next to it. They must "blend" the new paint into the adjacent panels to ensure a seamless color match. You will often see blending listed as a separate paint labour line item.

The Role of the Deductible

At the very bottom of the estimate, you will see the total repair cost including taxes and fees. If this is an at-fault Collision or Comprehensive claim, you will also see your deductible subtracted from the final total. The insurance company pays the body shop the remaining balance, and you are responsible for paying your deductible directly to the shop when you pick up the car.

The Part Most People Miss: Supplements

The biggest misunderstanding drivers have about repair estimates is assuming the first document they receive is the final price. It almost never is.

The initial estimate is typically written based only on visible damage. When the car arrives at the body shop and they begin "teardown" (taking the damaged panels off), they frequently find hidden structural damage underneath. Modern vehicles are packed with expensive parking sensors, radar units, and cameras behind the bumpers that cannot be assessed until the bumper is removed.

When this happens, the shop files a supplement—an updated estimate requesting more money from the insurance company to fix the newly discovered damage. Supplements are a completely normal part of the process, but they are also the primary cause of repair delays, especially if the shop has to wait for insurance approval or backordered OEM parts.

This teardown phase is also the exact moment when many seemingly repairable vehicles suddenly become a total loss. If a $4,000 initial estimate jumps to $9,000 after a supplement is filed, the repair cost might suddenly exceed the vehicle's Actual Cash Value (ACV) threshold. The insurer will immediately halt repairs, and your rental coverage timeline will suddenly become very urgent.

This is why understanding your vehicle's market value before the repair begins is so critical. If your older vehicle has borderline damage, knowing the ACV helps you mentally prepare for the very real possibility that a hidden supplement could trigger a total loss.

Frequently Asked Questions

Different shops may use different repair methodologies, have varying hourly labour rates, or source parts from different suppliers. One shop might write to replace a bumper entirely, while another believes they can repair the existing plastic. This is normal and usually requires the insurance adjuster to step in and agree on an approved repair plan.

In most standard auto insurance policies, the insurer has the right to use aftermarket or LKQ/recycled parts to restore your vehicle to its pre-accident condition, unless you specifically purchased an "OEM endorsement" on your policy. If you demand new OEM parts, you may have to pay the price difference out of pocket.
